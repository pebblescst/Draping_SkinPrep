Surgical Drapes

Standards I – IX: sterile draping requirements, handling rules, fluid resistance, flame resistance, drape characteristics, reusable woven drapes, laser precautions, and team selection involvement. Effective April 13, 2008.

🎯
Primary purpose: To isolate the surgical site from other areas of the patient's body and nonsterile areas of the OR table, contributing to the reduction of SSI risk. Drapes serve as a barrier to endogenous (patient's own skin flora — the major SSI source) and exogenous contamination. Draping also expands the sterile field, allowing the CST and sterile team to place instruments and supplies on the drapes, including establishing the neutral zone for passing sharps.

Standard I — Only sterile drapes in the sterile field

✅Sterile drape requirements
  • Only sterile drapes may be used within the sterile field
  • Must be properly sterilized per ANSI and AAMI recommendations — sterilization methods include radiation, steam, and ethylene oxide
  • Sterile team members must not come into contact with the contaminated undersurface of the drape that has contacted a nonsterile surface
  • Drapes should be handled as little as possible by sterile team members
  • Drapes should not be allowed to unfold before placement on the patient
  • Non-perforating towel clips only — perforating clips create holes that allow microorganism migration onto the sterile field from the patient's skin
  • Contaminated drapes at end of procedure: placed in impervious biohazard-labeled bags
  • Reusable woven drapes: follow manufacturer's care and reprocessing instructions
🚫Critical draping rules
  • Once positioned, a drape must never be repositioned — repositioning brings the nonsterile hanging portion into the sterile field, contaminating it and transferring microbes to the patient
  • The top of OR table, back table, and prep table is sterile; the portion hanging below the edge is nonsterile
  • CST, CSFA, and surgeon must maintain 12 inches from the OR table during the draping procedure — prevents contamination of sterile gloves and gown front
  • Must not reach across an undraped OR table to perform draping
  • When handing the four towels to the surgeon or CSFA for squaring off the incision site, the CST must stand on the same side of the OR table
  • Sterile gloved hands must be protected by "cuffing" during drape placement to prevent contamination

Standard II — Integrity of the microbial barrier

🚫
A compromise in integrity = contamination. Drapes must be free of holes, punctures, and tears. Drapes must be resistant to punctures and tears to prevent microbial contamination of the sterile field. Any breach in the drape barrier compromises the sterile field.

Standard III — Fluid resistance

💧Strike-through contamination prevention
  • Draping material must be impervious and fluid-resistant — prevents strike-through contamination from microorganisms; reducing strike-through reduces SSI risk
  • Blood, body fluids, and irrigating solutions must be suctioned and/or sponged as soon as possible from the drape surface
  • Impervious barriers incorporated into drapes, particularly around the fenestration, provide additional protection and increase resistance to strike-through
⚠️Drape placement and irrigation risk
  • When preparing the sterile back table and Mayo stand, do not place drapes underneath the basin
  • If the circulator pours irrigation solution into the basin before the patient is draped, spilled fluid can contact the drapes and cause strike-through contamination — rendering them useless
  • A replacement sterile drape would be required, increasing costs and disrupting the setup

Standard IV — Lint free

🔬Why lint-free drapes are required
  • Lint is recognized as a vector for causing SSI
  • Airborne lint serves as a transport medium for microbes
  • Lint-free drapes minimize airborne contamination and the spread of particles into the surgical wound

Standard V — Flame resistance

Standard V · Fire Safety
Drape flammability standards & fire prevention
▼
  • No local, state, or federal regulations currently establish performance standards specifically for surgical drapes; however, most manufacturers have adopted the same standards used for surgical gowns
  • NFPA No. 702-1980: Standard for Classification of the Flammability of Wearing Apparel — removed from NFPA's active list in 1987 but still used as a reference by manufacturers, FDA, and AAMI. Established four classes based on ignition and flame spread. Class 1 (relatively slow burn) is most relevant for surgical drapes
  • CPSC 16 CFR Part 1610: Standard for Flammability of Clothing Textiles. When evaluating a drape, confirm the manufacturer's documentation includes: "The Basic Fabric Meets the Class 1 Flammability Requirements for CPSC 16 CFR Part 1610"
  • Drapes must resist ignition from lasers, fiberoptics, and electrosurgery within the sterile field
  • Drape fires can cause serious injuries to both the patient and surgical team members
  • All surgical team members must be properly educated in fire prevention and healthcare facility protocol
  • All HCWs must complete annual fire safety competencies

Standard VI — Drape evaluation factors

📋Performance characteristics
  • Easy to handle and flexible — conforms to the contour of the patient and OR table as much as possible
  • Free of toxic substances such as dyes and laundry detergent residues (particularly reusable woven drapes)
  • Limited memory — draping material that retains a folded shape is difficult to work with and position properly
  • Fluid and microbial barrier evaluated using ASTM F1670 (synthetic blood penetration) and ASTM F1671 (bloodborne pathogen penetration) tests — the definitive tests per AAMI and FDA
  • Manufacturer must provide F1670/F1671 test results when drapes are being evaluated
📖Selection guidance — key references
  • AAMI TIR No. 11-1994 — Selection of Surgical Gowns and Drapes in Healthcare Facilities; highly recommended during evaluation
  • ANSI/AAMI PB70 — Liquid Barrier Performance and Classification of Protective Apparel and Drapes Intended for Use in Healthcare Facilities
  • Evaluate a variety of manufacturers' drapes and review documentation to verify ANSI/AAMI barrier performance standards are met
  • Surgical team members must be involved in the drape selection process

Standard VIII — Laser procedure draping

⚡Laser precautions during draping
  • Consider the use of aluminum-coated drapes for laser procedures
  • Currently, no draping material is 100% resistant to laser beam impact — precautions are always required regardless of drape type
  • Outline the surgical site with wet towels when draping for laser procedures
  • Refer to AST Standards of Practice for the Safe Use of Lasers for complete guidance

Skin Prep Technique

Standards I – IV: preoperative patient prep, hair removal, allergy screening, patient instructions, and standardized skin prep technique. Effective October 20, 2008.

🎯
Core purpose: The majority of SSIs are caused by the patient's own microbial flora entering the surgical wound. Since the patient's skin cannot be sterilized, skin prep removes debris, cleanses the skin, reduces resident and transient microbes to an irreducible minimum, and hinders microbial growth during surgery.

Standard I — Preoperative preparations

Standard I · Preop Shower
Preoperative patient bathing & CHG instructions
▼
  • Surgeon's orders may include the patient bathing or showering with an antiseptic agent the night before surgery and/or the morning of surgery
  • The CDC recommends requiring patients to bathe or shower with an antiseptic agent the night before surgery
  • CHG patient instructions — all three must be communicated:
    • CHG is inactivated by soaps and shampoos — the patient must ensure soap and shampoo are thoroughly rinsed off before using CHG
    • CHG is an eye irritant and can burn the corneas; it is also ototoxic — patient must keep CHG away from eyes and ears
    • Patient must not use body lotion after bathing with CHG — lotion inactivates the residual bacteriostatic effects of CHG
Standard I · Allergy Screening
Patient allergy interview & documentation
▼
  • The preoperative patient interview must include asking about known allergies and reviewing the patient's history and physical
  • Shellfish allergy (may contain iodine) → use a non-iodine prep solution; allergy to iodine is already indicated
  • Allergies to strawberries, bananas, kiwis, or poinsettias (contain latex elements) → document as latex allergic; perform shave and skin prep in a latex-free OR environment
  • Allergy to antiseptic solutions must be documented in the patient chart, on the cover of the patient chart, and on the patient allergy wristband worn on the day of surgery
  • Latex allergy must be documented the same way
  • The patient care plan must be revised to reflect the allergy and ensure the correct antiseptic agent is used or a latex-free environment is established
Standard I · Hair Removal
Shave prep — evidence & guidelines
▼
⚠️
Hair removal is not recommended. The surgical team should follow the surgeon's orders. If ordered, clippers are the preferred method — never a razor.
  • IHI, NPSF, and TMA (2006): promote elimination of surgical site shaving; if hair removal is necessary, perform with clippers right before surgery
  • Cochrane Database (11 RCTs): no difference in SSI rates between hair removal and no hair removal; clipping and depilatory creams result in fewer SSIs vs. razor shaving; no difference in SSI between shaving one day before vs. day of surgery
  • Cranial procedures: two studies found no infections or complications and no discernible increase in SSI when cranial surgery performed without hair removal; also provides positive psychological benefit to patients
  • Joanna Briggs Institute: surgery without hair removal is preferable; clipping is the overall preferred method if removal is necessary; perform as close to surgery time as possible (preferably less than 2 hours before surgery)
  • If ordered: performed in the preoperative holding area — not in the OR — to maintain patient privacy
  • Preferred method: electric clippers; second choice: depilatory cream (test on small skin patch first to check for reaction)
  • Follow manufacturer's instructions for cleaning and disinfecting reusable electric clippers; single-use shaving heads disposed of in a sharps container
  • Wet shave prep recommended — water softens hair and provides a smoother surface, reducing skin irritation and cuts
  • Craniotomy hair: placed in a secure labeled container (preferably ziplock-type bag labeled with patient name and facility ID number) — transported with the patient postoperatively, as hair is the patient's property
  • Loose hair collected with hypoallergenic tape or latex-free peel-and-stick mitt to prevent hair from entering the surgical wound
  • Eyebrows must never be shaved; if thick, carefully trimmed with small scissors. Long eyelashes carefully trimmed with small scissors
  • Patient education: instruct patient not to perform a self-shave prep or use a depilatory the night before or morning of surgery
  • Alternative for keeping hair out of cranial wounds without removal: apply non-flammable gel to the hair
Standard I · Additional Preop Prep
Jewelry, cosmetics, and nail requirements
▼
  • Body jewelry must be removed from the area of the skin prep — skin under jewelry is a source of high microbial counts; removal allows proper cleansing, prevents patient injury during positioning, prevents pooling of prep solutions, and prevents electrosurgical burns
  • Prior to skin prep, the pierced area should be cleaned
  • No cosmetics on the day of surgery — cosmetics can inhibit the effectiveness of the antiseptic solution
  • For finger, hand, or wrist surgery: patient instructed to cut nails short, thoroughly clean subungual areas during the preop bath or shower, remove artificial nails and nail polish

Standard IV — Standardized skin prep technique

📌

Most important principle: Skin prep always progresses from the clean area to the dirty area — begin at the planned incision site and carry to the periphery using an ever-widening circular motion. Once the periphery is reached, discard the sponge and do not bring it back over the clean area.

Perform a hand wash before performing the skin prep.

Remove gross soil, grease, skin oil, blood, and other debris from the skin prior to the skin prep using a non-aseptic, non-irritating, non-flammable, non-toxic fat solvent or degreaser.

Don sterile gloves. The skin prep is performed using sterile technique — avoid touching nonsterile items (OR bed sheets, blankets) with sterile gloves, prep solution sponges, or paint solution sponge sticks.

Apply sterile towels at the periphery of the skin prep boundaries to prevent accumulation of agents. Cover ECG leads, electrosurgery dispersive electrode (grounding pad), and tourniquet with an adhesive clear plastic drape to prevent prep fluid accumulation and chemical burns. If antiseptic solution contacts any of these, they must be removed and replaced.

Begin at the planned incision site and carry to the periphery using an ever-widening circular motion. Duration: minimum of 5 minutes and until all sponges have been used. Boundaries must be much wider than the planned incision — allows for incision lengthening, trocar placement, conversion from endoscopic to open procedure, and wound drain placement.

Discard each sponge once the periphery is reached. Never bring a sponge back over a previously cleaned area — it would transfer microbes from the dirty periphery to the clean incision site.

Apply paint solution with prep stick sponges using the no-touch technique — avoids contamination from gloves that contacted the prep-solution–soaked sponges.

Do not allow prep solution to pool under or adjacent to the patient — pooling can cause chemical burns and increases the risk of electrosurgical or laser ignition. At the end of the procedure, towels removed carefully to avoid dragging across the prep area.

✅Key technique reminders
  • Minimum duration: 5 minutes and until all sponges are used — ideal duration not definitively established, same as surgical scrub
  • Prep boundaries much wider than planned incision site
  • Use gentle pressure on patients with friable skin
  • Alcohol-based solution must be completely dry before placing drapes — fumes building up under drapes can ignite, particularly if ESU or laser will be used
🚫Absolute prohibitions
  • CHG, alcohol, and alcohol-based agents must not be used on mucous membranes
  • Prep solution must not be allowed to pool or accumulate under or adjacent to the patient
  • Antiseptic agents must always remain in the manufacturer's original container — never refill or transfer to a secondary container
  • Flammable solutions must never be warmed — warming makes chemicals volatile and unstable, creating a fire hazard

Special Situations

Standards V – VII: contaminated area sequencing, dual-site surgical preps, eye and facial preps, stoma management, and open wound preparation.

🎯
Core rule: Contaminated areas are always prepped last. The only exception is the umbilicus, which is prepped first to prevent debris from splashing onto the already-prepped abdomen. Always progress from clean to dirty.

Standard V — Contaminated area sequencing

⭐

Exception to the "dirty last" rule — the umbilicus: The umbilicus is contaminated, but whenever it is part of the skin prep it is prepped first, using prep-solution–soaked cotton-tipped applicators. This prevents debris from the umbilicus from splashing onto the prepped abdomen.

Areas always prepped last

!

Axilla, groin, perineal region, anus, and vagina — areas of high microbial counts; each sponge used and discarded, never reused

!

Stomas, skin ulcers, sinuses, and open wounds — considered contaminated; prepped last following clean-to-dirty principle

Stoma management

⚠️Stoma skin prep sequence

Isolate the stoma with a sterile clear plastic adhesive drape

If the stoma is part of the skin prep, cleanse it first — mucin and organic matter can inhibit antiseptic agent effectiveness; removal is necessary

Place a betadine-soaked sponge over the stoma after cleansing

Complete the skin prep of the surrounding area

Remove the sponge and prep the stoma last, following the clean-to-dirty principle — prep solution must not contaminate the previously prepped area

Open wound (traumatic) management

🩹Open wound prep sequence
  • May require extensive irrigation with warm sterile normal saline — bulb syringe for small wounds, pulse lavage for larger wounds
  • Place a moisture-proof pad under the patient before irrigation; dry towels or sheets may also be needed after
  • Surgeon may debride the open wound after irrigation
  • Prep the surrounding area first — pack the open wound with sterile gauze while surrounding skin is being prepped
  • Remove the sterile gauze and prep the open wound last

Penis prep

📋Foreskin management
  • Retract the foreskin (uncircumcised patients) and cleanse thoroughly
  • After cleansing, the foreskin must be placed back over the glans — avoids vascular disruption

Standard VI — Dual-site surgical preps

Standard VI · Graft Procedures
Skin, bone, or vascular graft — separate setups
▼
  • Separate skin prep setups are required for the donor and recipient sites
  • Donor site prepped first with a colorless solution, most often CHG — allows the surgeon to properly visualize the skin while taking the graft
  • Recipient site is often an open wound (e.g., a burn) and therefore is considered contaminated; must not be prepped with alcohol, alcohol-based agents, or povidone-iodine — these can cause chemical burns of the tissues
Standard VI · Dual-Site Preps
Abdominal-perineal & abdominal-vaginal procedures
▼
  • Two separate skin preps required — the perineal and vaginal areas are considered contaminated
  • Perineal or vaginal area prepped first — avoids splashing and contaminating the abdomen if it were prepped first
  • If using povidone-iodine: the mucous membranes of the vagina may only be prepped with the paint solution (not scrub solution)
  • After the perineum or vagina is prepped, the area must be covered with sterile towels during the abdominal prep

Standard VII — Eye and facial preps

🚫
CHG and iodophors are contraindicated for eye and facial preps. Both agents can severely injure the cornea if they accidentally enter the eye. Additionally, both are ototoxic and can cause sensorineural deafness if they enter the inner ear.
✅Safe agents for eye/facial preps
  • Triclosan and PCMX are considered non-toxic and should be used for facial preps
  • Still ensure agents do not enter the eyes even with non-toxic agents
  • Warm sterile water used as the rinse
🛡️Patient protection during eye/facial prep
  • If patient is awake: advise patient to keep eyes closed during the prep
  • If patient is under anesthesia: protect eyes by placing a small sterile plastic adhesive drape over the eyes
  • Place cotton balls in the ears to prevent prep agent from entering the inner ear

Agents & Documentation

Standards II–III & VIII–X: FDA antiseptic agent requirements, alcohol-based solutions, storage rules, OSHA MSDS requirements, and complete documentation standards.

🎯
Key principle: The healthcare facility must provide FDA-approved agents with immediate, cumulative, and persistent antimicrobial action. Surgical team members and the infection control officer must be involved in evaluating and selecting skin prep agents. Cost must not be a factor in the decision-making process.

Standard II — FDA requirements for antiseptic agents

🏛️Required properties — FDA Division of Over-the-Counter Drug Products
  • Substantially reduce transient microorganisms
  • Possess a broad-spectrum of antimicrobial properties
  • Be fast-acting
  • Provide persistent, cumulative activity
  • Be nonirritating to the skin

Standard III — Alcohol as an antiseptic agent

⚠️
Alcohol must not be used as the sole skin prep agent. Alcohol has rapid activity but no persistent, cumulative activity when used alone. It must always be used as part of a combination regimen (e.g., alcohol + CHG).
🔬Antimicrobial properties
  • Mechanism: denaturing of proteins
  • 60%–95% alcohol is most effective — higher concentrations are less effective because protein denaturing does not occur easily without water
  • Broad-spectrum: destroys Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, MRSA, VRE, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and fungi
  • Rapid activity when applied to skin
  • Alone: no persistent or cumulative activity — must be combined with another antiseptic agent
  • Alcohol-based solutions immediately lower microbial skin counts more effectively than other solutions
📊Agent effectiveness ranking
  • #1 — Alcohol-based solution with 0.5%–1% CHG: persistent activity equal to or greater than CHG alone; most effective overall
  • #2 — Chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) alone
  • #3 — Iodophors
  • #4 — Triclosan
  • #5 — PCMX (parachlorometaxylenol) — contradictory study results; further research required
  • An alcohol wipe after the paint solution has been applied is acceptable — alcohol is being used as part of the overall skin prep regimen
Standard III · Usage Restrictions
When alcohol-based solutions cannot be used
▼
  • Must not be used when the patient's skin is visibly dirty or contaminated with proteinaceous materials — proteinaceous matter decreases the antimicrobial action; prewash with non-antimicrobial soap and dry thoroughly before applying
  • Must not be used on mucous membranes — CHG, alcohol, and alcohol-based agents are all contraindicated on mucous membranes
  • Open wounds / recipient graft sites — must not be prepped with alcohol, alcohol-based agents, or povidone-iodine (risk of chemical burns to tissues)
  • Must be completely dry before draping — fumes can build up under drapes and be ignited by ESU or laser
  • Follow manufacturer's instructions for application — instructions vary by solution

Standard VIII — Storage of antiseptic agents

📦Storage requirements
  • Always stored in the manufacturer's original container
  • Container must never be refilled or agent transferred to a secondary container — contributes to microbial contamination of original and/or secondary container
  • Single-use containers are recommended
  • Alcohol and alcohol-containing solutions stored in a dry, cool area approved for flammable storage — away from flames, heating vents, and high temperatures; per NFPA and local/state regulations
🌡️Warming of antiseptic solutions
  • Follow manufacturer's instructions before warming any antiseptic solution
  • Flammable solutions must never be warmed — chemicals become volatile and unstable; easily catches fire
  • Warming can change the chemical properties of a solution, reducing its microbial effectiveness
  • If warmed, ensure it is not too hot — risk of patient burn

Standard IX — MSDS / Safety Data Sheets

⚠️OSHA mandate — Material Safety Data Sheets
  • As mandated by OSHA, MSDSs for all antiseptic agents and chemicals stored and used in the surgery department must be readily available and accessible to all surgical personnel
  • If unsure about handling, storage, use, or disposal of any agent or chemical, surgical personnel must review the MSDS and follow the instructions

Key terms glossary

TermDefinition
Endogenous contaminationContamination originating from the patient's own body — primarily the patient's skin flora; identified as the major source of SSI. The primary reason draping and skin prep are performed.
Exogenous contaminationContamination from external sources — surgical team members and the OR environment. Drapes serve as a barrier to both endogenous and exogenous sources.
Strike-through contaminationContamination occurring when fluid passes through a drape's microbial barrier, carrying microorganisms through to the sterile side. Prevented by impervious, fluid-resistant draping material.
FenestrationThe opening cut into a surgical drape that exposes the operative site; impervious barriers around the fenestration provide added protection against strike-through contamination.
CuffingFolding back the edge of a drape over the sterile gloved hands to protect against contamination when placing drapes on the patient.
CHG (Chlorhexidine gluconate)A broad-spectrum antiseptic agent; inactivated by soaps/shampoos; ototoxic and cornea-irritating — contraindicated on mucous membranes and near eyes/ears.
No-touch techniqueMethod of applying paint solution using prep stick sponges without the gloves (which touched the prep-solution–soaked sponges) contacting the previously prepped skin; prevents contamination.
Friable skinSkin that is fragile and easily damaged; gentle pressure must be used when applying prep agents to avoid injury.
Limited memory (drapes)A desirable drape characteristic — draping material that does not retain its folded shape, making it easier to handle and conform to patient and table contours.
ASTM F1670 / F1671ASTM test methods for assessing fluid and microbial barriers of surgical fabrics; F1670 tests resistance to synthetic blood; F1671 tests resistance to bloodborne pathogen penetration. FDA and AAMI confirm these are the definitive barrier tests.
MSDSMaterial Safety Data Sheet — OSHA-mandated document for all chemicals and antiseptic agents used in the surgery department; must be readily accessible to all surgical personnel.
Depilatory creamA chemical hair removal product; the second choice for hair removal after electric clippers; must be tested on a small skin patch before use on a larger area to check for patient reaction.
Pulse lavageA method of wound irrigation using pulsating pressurized saline; used for large traumatic open wounds to remove debris before skin prep of the surrounding area.
Subungual areaThe area beneath the fingernail — a source of high microbial counts; patients undergoing hand/finger/wrist surgery instructed to thoroughly clean this area during the preop shower.
